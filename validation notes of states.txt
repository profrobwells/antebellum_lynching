The Arkansas Intelligencer, on September 1, 1849, editorialized against lynching saying it was bad for the state's reputation: "The murder of Mr. A. was one of the most atrocious and cold-blooded deeds that
we have ever been called on to record, and it demanded, in the strongest terms,
the severest penalty of the offended law. But did the enormity of his crime
justify a departure from the due course of law and a resort to Lynch law? We
think not; and we are sorry to see such manifestations of the popular will, in
any case, especially where the laws of our country have provided an adequate
and proper punishment. The presses of other states have always taken peculiar
delight in harping upon the morals of our people, and we fear that the recent
occurrence at Bentonville will be caught up as another instance of the laxity
of morals in Arkansas."



The Washington Telegraph reported on April 9, 1851 about an abolitiionist being lynched in Kentucky. "Rev. Edward Mathews, traveling agent of the American Baptist
Emancipation Society of New York, while on a pilgrimage to the residence of
Cassius M. Clay of Kentucky, stopped at Richmond, Kentucky, and made use of
some certain unwholesome expressions relative to the subject of slavery, which
induced the citizens to order him to leave the town. He left but returned in a
day or two afterwards, whereupon some citizens seized him, and after ducking
him nine times in a horse pond, ordered him to leave the State."

incensed, and when the train halted at Jackson, jumped to the platform and
